  1. What is "Aposteme"?

 **Phonetic/Textual Correction:** The word in the text is **aposteme**.
 **General Meaning:** In a classical philosophical context, *aposteme* generally refers to a kind of severance, detachment, or deviation from one's proper natural state or place.

 ### 2. Contextual Meaning (Within this Passage)

In Section XIV, the author uses "aposteme" in connection with the soul wronging itself:

  "A man's soul doth wrong and disrespect itself first and especially, when as much as in itself lies it becomes an **aposteme**, and as it were an excrescency of the world, for to be grieved and displeased with anything that happens in the world, is direct apostacy from the nature of the universe..."

 **In this specific context, becoming "an aposteme" means:**

1.  **Deviation from Nature:** The soul ceases to live according to natural law or reason.
2.  **Self-Exile/Estrangement:** By allowing itself to be intensely grieved, displeased, or attached to external events (like worldly goods, slights, or pleasures), the soul is acting as if it does not belong within the naturally ordered cosmos described by the universe.

 **In simpler terms, to become "an aposteme" in this passage means that the soul has rejected its natural place of tranquility and acceptance, instead attaching itself to passing, external things.** This emotional reaction (grief over externals) is seen by the author as a profound form of self-disrespect or cosmic rebellion.

Here is a summary of the overarching principles presented in this body of work:

 

  Overarching Themes (The Mandate for the Virtuous Life)

 **1. Primacy of Reason over External Reward:**
The text warns against allowing worldly desires—be it praise, wealth, pleasure, or status—to overpower rational judgment. True virtue is not determined by external accolades but must be internally consistent and rationally profitable. The focus must remain on the development of the rational soul rather than accommodating social appearance.

 **2. Cultivating Inner Integrity and Indifference:**
The highest ethical goal is to achieve a state of *ataraxia* (freedom from emotional disturbance). A disciplined mind should be immune to anxiety, fear, intense longing, or hatred. The individual must learn to accept their lot—whether life or death—with total composure, maintaining purity in thought and action at all times.

 **3. The Necessity of Constant Scrutiny:**
The core intellectual exercise is deep contemplation (*self-examination*). One must apply the "opinative faculty" (reason) with relentless thoroughness to every idea, event, or object that presents itself. This involves:
    Dissecting things into their constituent parts.
 *   Naming the true nature and function of everything observed.
 *   Asking profound questions: What is its purpose? How does it connect to the greater universe?

 **4. Living in the Moment (Memento Mori):**
A central caution repeated throughout is the fleeting nature of existence—time, life itself, fame, and physical location are momentary blips. This realization should instill an urgency to live virtuously *now*, without procrastination or the delusion that one will have "more time" for reflection later in life.

 **5. Ethical Connection (The Interrelation):**
All actions must be viewed through two lenses: their connection to the divine/universal law, and their necessary interaction with human society ("neighbor," "kinsman"). One cannot act justly without considering this relationship between the internal spirit and the external world.

 ***

 In essence, the philosophy laid out is a comprehensive guide to achieving moral fortitude by:**
    Constantly using your highest reason.
 *   Detaching yourself from dependency on pleasure or fear.
 *   Maintaining an intellectually humble skepticism toward all worldly claims.
 *   Acting justly and kindly in every moment, fully aware that life's greatest gift is the present instant.
