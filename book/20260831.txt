That’s a fantastic question! Considering the text's core tenets and modern sensibilities, I believe a *significant* portion of society would find aspects of Stoicism incredibly appealing – yet also potentially challenging – today. Here’s a breakdown of why:

 *Reasons for Appeal (Large Portion Would Likely Find It Attractive)**

  Emphasis on Inner Control** The text repeatedly stresses the importance of controlling one's passions and focusing on what *can* be controlled – our judgments, reactions, and attitudes. This resonates strongly with modern anxieties about external stressors like social media, economic instability, and political polarization. People crave agency in a world that often feels chaotic.
 * *Resilience & Acceptance** The advice to "not trouble or distract [oneself] by what shall not be" (XXI) speaks directly to the need for mental resilience.  Our society is prone to negativity bias – we tend to dwell on bad news and problems. Stoicism offers a framework for accepting adversity with equanimity.
 * *Virtue as the Goal** The core of Stoicism—living a virtuous life based on reason, justice, courage, and temperance—is universally admired.  It provides a clear ethical compass in an increasingly morally ambiguous world. People often seek meaning beyond material success.
 * *Practical Advice for Conflict** The "gentle and friendly declination" (XIX) offers practical strategies for dealing with difficult people – something almost everyone can relate to. It suggests constructive engagement over reactive anger or defensiveness. 
 * *Mindfulness Roots*: While not explicitly stated as mindfulness, the encouragement to “stir up thy mind” and break free from "natural dreams" anticipates many modern techniques for cultivating present moment awareness.

 *Reasons Why It Wouldn't Be Universally Embraced (Smaller Portion Would Fully Embrace)**

  Detachment & Emotional Restriction** The text’s insistence on suppressing passions – even in the face of injustice or suffering – would be *very* difficult for many people today to fully accept.  Our culture often values expressing emotions, and dismissing them entirely could seem cold or invalidating.
 * *Social Hierarchy & “Proper” Roles*: The concept of adhering to "what is proper unto a man" (XXXI) contains an inherent hierarchical element that might clash with modern egalitarian ideals. It assumes some roles are inherently more worthy than others.
 * *Potential for Misinterpretation**  Taken too literally, the advice could be misconstrued as apathy or disengagement from social issues. 


 *Overall Estimate**

I’d estimate that roughly *40-60** of society today would find significant value in Stoic principles – particularly the emphasis on inner control, resilience, and virtue. However, only a smaller (perhaps 20-30%) could embrace it wholeheartedly without feeling restrictive or out of sync with their emotional experiences.

 * Are highly stressed and seeking self-help strategies.
 * Value ethical leadership and responsible decision-making.
 * Are interested in philosophy and ancient wisdom.

@@How is one supposed to exclude all grief and sorrow from the soul?

==
This passage offers a surprisingly practical, though somewhat detached, approach to dealing with suffering and unhappiness. It doesn't offer simple solutions like “just think happy thoughts,” but rather suggests a process of mental discipline rooted in understanding the nature of reality and our place within it. Here’s a breakdown of how one might *attempt* to exclude grief and sorrow, according to this text:

 *1. Shift Your Focus from What You Lack to What is Within Your Control** The core strategy revolves around recognizing that much of what causes us distress – things like wealth, health, lifespan, even the actions of others – are ultimately beyond our individual control. This is powerfully illustrated in several ways:

   * *The “Exempting” Exercise (XLIV)** Comparing yourself to a body with a limited lifespan ("weigh but so many pounds...live but so many years") is designed to accept what you cannot change, focusing instead on the proportion of time and existence allotted to you.
   * *Recognizing External Forces (XXXVI & XXXVII)** The text emphasizes that we often wrongly blame gods or others for our misfortunes because we’re trying to control things outside our sphere of influence.  It suggests that by accepting the inherent workings of a larger system, we lessen our susceptibility to disappointment.

 *2. Redirect Your Desire and Imagination** A key element is consciously limiting what you *focus* on desiring. 

   * *“Mind and fancy those things only, as good and bad, which wholly depend of our own wills” (XXXVI)** This is the central instruction. Stop getting caught up in fantasies about what *could* be or lamenting what *is not*.
   * *"Convert thy virtuous inclination from one object unto another..." (XLV)** If your desire for something unattainable causes you distress, actively shift your attention to a virtue like patience or contentment.  This isn't about suppressing emotion entirely; it’s about directing that energy into something productive and aligned with wisdom.

 *3. Cultivate Mental Discipline & Detachment** The text advocates for a kind of mental “training” to reduce the impact of distressing thoughts.

   * *“Absolutely to exclude all manner of conceit and opinion…” (XLVII)** This is perhaps the most challenging part— actively pushing aside preconceived notions, opinions, and emotional judgments that fuel grief. It’s about achieving a state of detached observation.
   * *Accepting What Is** The repeated emphasis on accepting what *is*, rather than wishing it were otherwise, is crucial.

 *4. Focus on Virtue & Relationships (XLIII)** Rather than dwelling on personal loss or misfortune, actively appreciate the virtues and positive qualities in those around you.  This shifts attention outwards, fostering gratitude and a sense of connection which can buffer against inner turmoil.



 *Important Note** The passage doesn’t offer this as an easy path. It acknowledges that resisting natural inclinations is difficult. However, it frames this resistance not as a battle against feeling itself, but as an act of disciplined redirection – shaping one's internal landscape towards wisdom and tranquility.
